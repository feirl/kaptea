Should you introduce an AI receptionist?

13 Aug 2026 · 9 min read · Alan McNab
The short answer

For most of what a service business is asked on the phone, AI is already good enough, and memory and context are closing the gap on the rest. But "should we introduce an AI receptionist" is the wrong question. The receptionist is one participant in a customer journey, the failures happen at the handoffs between participants, and the only honest measure is whether the promise made to the customer was kept. Evaluate the promise, not the receptionist.

Published containment rates for voice AI run anywhere from 5% to 90%. That spread should tell you something: the technology is rarely what decides the outcome.

I get asked this most weeks now, usually in this form: is the AI good enough yet to answer our phones?

It is a fair question and it has a short answer. For most of what a service business gets asked on the phone, yes. For anything with real complexity, the gap is closing quickly, and memory and context are what is closing it.

But it is the wrong question, and I want to explain why, because the businesses that get this right are asking something else entirely.

What the published numbers actually say

Start with what is known, because the published figures are wilder than they look.

Traditional menu-driven IVR resolves somewhere around 5% to 10% of calls without a person. Systems built on older conversational AI tooling manage 10% to 15%. Modern agentic voice systems are quoted at 60% to 90%. Those particular bands come from Teneo's own published guidance, and it is worth saying plainly that most figures in this market are published by the companies selling the software, mine included.

The independent picture is harder to find than it should be. Almost every published figure in this market comes from a company selling the software, and the few genuinely independent studies are small. That absence is itself worth noticing: a category this large should have better numbers by now. So the honest range is enormous, and the same software sits at both ends of it. That alone should tell you the deciding variable is not the model.

The metric nearly everyone quotes is the wrong one

Here is the part that matters, and it is hiding in plain sight in the industry's own definitions.

Containment rate measures how many calls did not reach a human being. That is a measure of your team being left alone. It is not a measure of your customer being served.

A call that gets answered, understood, and quietly resolved counts as contained. So does a call where the caller gave up. So does a call where the agent said it would arrange something and nothing happened afterwards. All three look identical on the dashboard, and only one of them is a business outcome.

Even the industry's own guidance now says so. Teneo's benchmark note warns against prioritising raw containment over resolution quality. Twilio and Coval, writing on how to evaluate voice AI agents, argue that evaluation should be business-first: resolution rate, first-call resolution, booking completion, customer effort. Their framing is that technical metrics like latency, and they put human-normal latency at roughly 300 to 1,200 milliseconds, are diagnostics rather than goals.

I would go one step further. Resolution is measured at the moment the call ends. The promise made on that call usually comes due later.

Why the pilots disappoint, and it is not the AI

When an AI receptionist project underwhelms, the post-mortem almost always blames the voice. In my experience it is nearly always the handoffs.

There are four of them, and every one of them breaks in a different way:

  • Agentic to human. The call transfers and the customer starts again from the beginning. This is the one everybody has personally suffered.
  • Human to system. Your person makes a commitment and it lives in their head, or a notepad, or a follow-up they meant to send.
  • Agentic to agentic. One agent takes the booking, another handles the change, and neither knows what the other agreed.
  • Human to human. The oldest failure in service, and still the most common. The engineer knows, the office does not.

Every one of those is a promise crossing a boundary. The promise is where the value sits, and the boundary is where it falls over. You are not buying a receptionist. You are adding a participant to a chain that already has handoffs in it, and if the chain leaks today, the new participant will leak faster and at greater volume.

This is critical customer journey orchestration

Every one of those handoffs sits inside a single customer journey: the reservation, the change, the follow-up, the promise that something will happen on Thursday. Handle the receptionist in isolation and you have automated one step of that journey. Handle the journey end to end, across your human, agentic and automated workflows, and you have done the thing the whole category has been circling all year.

This is where the real conversation has moved, and it has a name. Orchestrating critical customer journeys across a combined human and agentic team is the point, and the receptionist is simply one voice within it. The idea is not new. What has changed is that you can now put it live, and two things separate the businesses that do it well.

The first is that it is built with ease. You stand up one specific, well-understood use case, watch what it does, and expand once the business trusts it. The second is that it is measured on trust. You track whether the promise made in each journey was kept, rather than whether a call avoided a human. Ease gets you live. Trust tells you it is working. Both are choices you make when you buy, and both matter far more than the model underneath.

How much does an AI receptionist cost?

People usually open this conversation by asking what it costs to deploy. That is the least interesting number in the discussion.

Look at the other side of the ledger first. In hospitality, QSR Magazine has put the industry's annual losses from unanswered phones at around $20 billion. An analysis by Slang AI found restaurants missing an average of 34% of calls, with 71% of calls being revenue-related. The Washington Hospitality Association's figure is that roughly 60% of missed calls are genuine customer intent, somebody trying to book or order.

Those are averages. Here is a specific one. A restaurant group we work with is losing in the region of one million euro a year in bookings that were never taken, because the phone rang while the team was serving the room. Nobody in that business was doing anything wrong. The calls simply arrive at the exact moment the floor is busiest.

Set the cost of orchestrating conversations across a combined human and agentic team against a number like that, and the deployment question answers itself. It is a fraction. For premium service businesses, where a customer is worth thousands a year and the whole proposition is that you look after people properly, the return is real. If you are choosing on price per minute, you are measuring the wrong side of the ledger.

Do AI receptionists actually work?

Being straight about capability, since the market rarely is.

For a straightforward reservation, an AI agent has access to the same system and the same availability as your team, and it performs on a par with a person. It also does it at three in the morning, and during the Friday rush, and on all six lines at once.

For complex requests, a person is still better, and the gap is narrowing faster than most people realise. Memory and context are the reason. An agent that recalls the last four interactions and carries them into this one behaves very differently from one meeting you for the first time on every call.

And the deployment reality has changed underneath the debate. Time to live is now measured in days. Building incrementally used to be impossible because every step cost months. That constraint has gone, and hardly anyone has adjusted their thinking to it yet.

So how do you actually decide?

Stop evaluating the receptionist. Evaluate the promise.

Every customer interaction contains one: someone will call back, a table is held, an engineer will come on Thursday, the part is ordered. That promise has a life of its own. It gets made, it gets delivered, it gets executed, and eventually the customer either confirms it happened or discovers that it did not.

That is measurable, and it is what we measure. We call it a trust score: whether the promise made in a customer journey was made clearly, delivered, and executed, tracked across every interaction. And because it is a real measure rather than a vanity one, it can be read by customer, by customer segment, by agent, and by request type.

Which turns the original question into something far more useful. Instead of asking whether AI is good enough to answer the phone, you can ask:

  • Which request types do we keep our promises on, and which do we not?
  • Do our agentic interactions keep promises at the same rate as our human ones?
  • Which segment of customers is quietly experiencing the broken ones?
  • Where in the chain does the promise actually fall over?

Answer those and you will know whether your receptionist is working, and it will not matter in the slightest whether that receptionist is a person or software. That is the point.

The honest pros and cons

When should you introduce an AI receptionist?

Introduce one when you are losing revenue to unanswered calls, your inbound is largely repeatable, you can name the specific use case to start with, and you can see the customer journey the call belongs to.

When should you wait?

Wait when you cannot yet see where your promises break, or your handoffs are already failing between the people you have. Adding a fast participant to a leaking chain increases the rate of the leak. Fix the chain, then add the speed.

Measure it on outcomes from day one.

Containment will make you feel good. Kept promises will tell you the truth.
